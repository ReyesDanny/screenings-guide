Explaining Screenings and Exams to Parents

A quick reference for Family Services and health staff: what each screening or exam is, why it matters, how it's done, what results mean, and plain-language words you can use with families in English or Spanish.

A simple flow for any screening conversation

  1. Start with the child. Share something positive you've noticed. Families listen better when they know you see their child's strengths.
  2. Say what it is in one sentence. "A vision screening is a quick check to see if your child's eyes need a closer look."
  3. Say why it matters for learning and growing. Connect it to things parents care about: talking, playing, getting ready for kindergarten.
  4. Say what to expect. How long, whether it hurts, what the parent can do to help their child feel safe.
  5. Explain results honestly and calmly. A screening is not a diagnosis. "Needs a second look" is not "something is wrong."
  6. Check understanding. Ask the parent to tell you, in their own words, what happens next (teach-back).
  7. Offer concrete help. Finding a provider, insurance, making the appointment, transportation, interpretation, reminders.

Words that help

Instead of…
Try…
"Your child failed the hearing test."
"We'd like to check again, and maybe have the doctor take a closer look at your child's ears."
"Your child is obese / too skinny."
"Let's look at how your child is growing over time and talk about what's working at home."
"The result was abnormal."
"The number was outside the usual range, so the next step is…"
"You have to get this done by Friday."
"This helps us make sure nothing gets in the way of learning. What would make it easier to get the appointment?"
"Lead poisoning"
"Lead in the blood" (then explain what level and what's next)
"Developmental delay"
"Some areas where a specialist could give extra support"

Respect, language, and culture

  • Offer communication in the family's preferred language. Use trained interpreters for results and referrals, not the child or siblings.
  • Ask what the family already knows and believes about the screening. Some families may have had painful experiences with clinics, blood draws, or immigration-related worries; listen first.
  • Home remedies and imported products can be important to families. Ask about them with curiosity, not judgment, especially for lead and nutrition.
  • Share results privately. Child health information is protected; follow your program's confidentiality and consent procedures.
  • Parents decide. Explain the benefits clearly, document the conversation, and keep the door open if a family declines.

After the conversation

  • Document the contact, the result or referral, and the agreed next step in C-FIT according to Centro procedure.
  • Set a reminder to follow up. Head Start requires tracking referrals until services are received or a plan is in place.
  • Loop in the Health & Nutrition team for anything urgent: very high lead, signs of tooth infection, a child who seems to be in pain, or a family that can't get care.
  • Celebrate completions with the family. A finished dental treatment or a new pair of glasses is a big deal.
